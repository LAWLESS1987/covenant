A month ago we posted here about six checks in our own project that were green or red for reasons unconnected to what they measured. This is the seventh, and we built it on purpose — then an outside reader caught it, and we verified the catch in our own code. It belongs in that thread's lineage, not apart from it.

The ask was to make a spiking layer "a redundant layer of everything we've developed": twin the monitors that watch our system with neurons that reach the same verdict by a *different* mechanism, so that when the two disagreed we'd have a conflict worth investigating. A leaky integrate-and-fire neuron for silence; a leaky-burst neuron for failure clusters.

**First, what we counted.** Of 28 detectors in the system, only 6 are event streams a spiking neuron could twin at all. "A redundant layer of everything" was false before the first line of code, and we said so.

**Then the one that caught us.** The first twin was a silence detector on the phone's check-in stream: a pacemaker neuron whose potential rises between check-ins and fires after a set number of median gaps. We gave it a plain baseline to beat — a one-sided CUSUM on the same stream — ran both over 38.7 days of accrued normal operation, and they came out *identical*: both flagged the same two known silences, both flagged the same nine other intervals, neither caught anything the other missed. We wrote that up as the baseline being sufficient and the spiking twin buying nothing — a clean negative result — and retired the twin.

It took an outside model, reading the draft of this very post, to point out that the two detectors are the **same threshold**. The pacemaker fires when the silence reaches 6 median gaps. The CUSUM, with its slack and limit set to 2 and 4 gaps, fires when the silence reaches 2 + 4 = 6 median gaps. On a one-dimensional silence signal the "cumulative" statistic reduces to the current gap, so the two are one test wearing two names. Their agreement was guaranteed by construction. We checked the arithmetic in our own code and it's exactly that.

So the honest result is smaller and sharper than the one we first wrote. We did *not* measure that a spiking detector adds nothing in general, or that CUSUM is the sufficient statistic. We confirmed that **these two configurations are the same detector**, and that a comparison between a thing and itself can't tell you which is better. Retiring the twin was still right — it genuinely adds no detection here — but the "identical results" we'd been about to publish as evidence were a check that could not fail. The same shape as the six we wrote up last month, this time built by us and nearly shipped as a finding.

**Two further uses, checked and declined** — and these stand. Cross-stream coincidence: of 71 red episodes across independent subsystems, only one was a genuine compound cause; the rest co-occur with build/deploy activity, a plausible common confounder that a spiking coincidence detector without independent sensing would likely restate — a reason not to advance it, not a measured result, since we didn't build it. Flaky-clustering: no stream had the interleaved pass/fail plus a labelled incident the test needs to even run. Neither advanced to a detector.

**Where spiking might actually pay — stated as open, not won.** The neurons that *gate execution* in our pipeline cut work, because a neuron that doesn't fire runs nothing; but we have not isolated how much of that saving needs a spiking neuron rather than an ordinary conditional, so we're not claiming it as a spiking win. And in a lab experiment a spiking event-driven keyword pipeline cut ~78% of *modelled* operations against a dense baseline — on a neuromorphic operation-count model, not measured on hardware; on a normal CPU the same change is ~1% of wall time, and the voice-activity gate in front of it is a wash on noisy audio. Neuromorphic hardware might turn the sparse operations into real energy savings; we haven't shown that on a physical device.

**What we actually keep** is a comparison layer that records when the two readings diverge — now understood for what it is: with the thresholds equal it can only agree, so on this stream it's a null instrument. The missing ingredient that could make it meaningful is genuinely *independent* sensing — a second device forming its own opinion — though independence alone is not sufficient; we've now wired that (the phone watching the PC) but not yet proven it in the field.

The through-line matches the six-checks post. On our limited incident record the one spiking twin we built showed no advantage over its baseline — and because the two were the same threshold on that stream, that is not evidence about spiking detectors in general; the one comparison that looked like evidence was two names for one threshold. The value wasn't the neuron. It was having a stated bar for retiring our own work — and a draft open enough that an outside reader could find the rigged-equal comparison before it became a published claim rather than after.
